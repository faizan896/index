City Council Approves Waterfront Housing Plan After Six-Hour Debate

Posted on May 14, 2024May 14, 2024 by Maria Lopez

After more than six hours of public comment and debate, the City Council voted 7-2 late Monday night to approve a plan for 1,150 homes on the former Bayview shipyard, ending a fight over the waterfront site that has lasted most of a decade.

The plan, submitted by a partnership between the Port Authority and two regional developers, sets aside 230 of the units for households earning less than 80 percent of the area median income. It also includes a two-acre public park, a ferry landing and a requirement that the ground floors along the new harbor walk be leased to small businesses rather than chain retailers.

“This is the most significant housing decision this council has made in a generation,” said Council President Denise Hartwell, who voted in favor. “We have spent years talking about the shortage. Tonight we did something about it.”

The two votes against came from council members representing the neighborhoods closest to the site. Councilmember Tom Achebe said he supported new housing in principle but worried that the plan’s traffic study underestimated how many cars the development would add to Shoreline Avenue, which already backs up during the morning commute.

“I want these homes built,” Achebe said. “I also want the people who live there to be able to get to work.”

A long road to a vote

The shipyard closed in 2009, and the city bought the land three years later with the intention of turning it into a mixed-use neighborhood. Two earlier proposals collapsed, one during the 2015 downturn in the regional construction market and the other after a lawsuit over contaminated soil near the dry docks.

Cleanup of the site was completed last year at a cost of roughly $38 million, most of it covered by state and federal grants. Environmental groups that sued over the earlier plan said Monday that they were satisfied with the remediation and would not challenge the new proposal.

Residents who spoke during public comment were divided. Several renters described paying more than half of their income for housing and urged the council to approve the plan without further delay. Some homeowners from the nearby Fairhaven neighborhood asked for the building heights along the water to be reduced from eight stories to five, arguing that taller buildings would block views from the hill.

The council rejected an amendment to lower the heights, but approved another that requires the developers to fund an additional bus line for the first five years after the first buildings open.

What happens next

The developers expect to break ground on the first phase, about 400 homes and the park, next spring. Construction of the full project is expected to take eight to ten years. The Port Authority will retain ownership of the land and lease it to the developers for 99 years, an arrangement that city officials said would give the public a share of rising land values over time.

The affordable units will be distributed throughout the buildings rather than concentrated in a single tower, a condition that housing advocates had pushed for since the earliest drafts of the plan.

Hartwell said the council would hold quarterly hearings on the project’s progress. “We will be watching every phase,” she said.
