It’s Complicated: Café Culture & the “Third Place”

by Zoë Black · October 3, 2022

In 1989 the sociologist Ray Oldenburg described what he called the “third place”: somewhere that is neither home (the first place) nor work (the second), where people gather on neutral ground, talk is the main activity, and the regulars set the tone. His examples were taverns, barbershops, post offices and, above all, cafés.

Thirty years later the café is everywhere and the third place seems harder to find. The room is full but everyone is wearing headphones. The tables are sized for a laptop and a single cup. The “neutral ground” now has a minimum spend — and a Wi‑Fi password that expires after an hour.

It would be easy to blame the chains, or the laptops, or the price of rent. But I think the story is more complicated & more interesting than that. People still want somewhere to be among others without being obliged to anyone. What has changed is how they want to spend that time: often alone, in company. The headphone-wearing writer in the corner is not rejecting the room; she is using it exactly as she wants to.

Oldenburg would probably not approve. For him the point was conversation, and conversation needs a certain kind of furniture: long tables, a counter you can lean on, staff who know your name. The good news is that those things still exist. They are just as likely to be found in a library, a launderette or a climbing gym as in a café.

So perhaps the question is not whether the café is still a third place, but where the third places have gone … and whether we have noticed them yet. In the next essay I will visit a few of them — starting with a laundrette in Leeds that hosts a weekly chess club.
