On Keeping a Commonplace Book

March 2021

For about twelve years I have kept a commonplace book: a single notebook, and later a single text file, into which I copy passages that I want to remember. It is the oldest habit I have that is still useful, older than any of the software I use to manage it, and I think it has shaped the way I read more than anything else.

The idea is not mine. Students in the Renaissance were taught to keep one, sorting quotations under headings like Friendship or Fortune so that they could find an apt line when they needed to write or speak. Locke published a method for indexing them. Plenty of writers whose books I love kept them, and some of those notebooks were later published as books in their own right.

My version is less organised. I copy the passage, write down where it came from, and add a line or two about why it caught my attention. There are no headings. Once a year I read the whole thing from the beginning, which takes a weekend, and I am always surprised by what I find. Passages I copied with great excitement often seem flat. Others that I barely remember copying turn out to be the ones I have been thinking about ever since.

The act of copying matters more than the archive. Writing a sentence out by hand, or even retyping it, forces me to slow down and notice its shape: where the commas fall, which word carries the weight, what the writer chose to leave out. I read more carefully when I know I might copy something, in the same way that I look more carefully at a landscape when I am carrying a camera.

There is also a quieter benefit. A commonplace book is a record of what I was paying attention to at a given time, and reading old entries is a way of meeting a previous version of myself. Some of those versions I am fond of. Others were preoccupied with things that no longer matter to me at all, which is reassuring in its own way.

If you want to start one, my only advice is to keep it in a single place and to make adding to it as easy as possible. Any system that requires a decision before you can write something down will eventually be abandoned. The sorting can happen later, or never.
